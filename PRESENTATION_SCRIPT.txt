SLIDE 1: Marriage and Divorce in America
============================================================
Hi, my name is Susana Fernandez Rivera. This is my DATA 201
capstone project. I looked at marriage and divorce in
America: how the rates have changed over time, whether 2019
was any different, and what divorced people have in common.
I used Python, pandas, matplotlib, and scipy for everything
you are about to see.

============================================================
SLIDE 2: Three research questions
============================================================
I had three research questions. One: how have marriage and
divorce rates changed over time, and how does New York
compare to the rest of the country? Two: was 2019 different
from the trend? And three: what do divorced people have in
common? The first question uses national and state data over
almost twenty years. The second and third use a survey of
New Yorkers from 2019.

============================================================
SLIDE 3: Two data sources
============================================================
I used two data sources. The first is from the CDC: national
marriage and divorce rates from 2000 to 2017, and rates for
every state going back to 1990. These came as Excel files
with footnotes, blank rows, and missing values that I had to
clean. The second is the Census Bureau's American Community
Survey for 2019, for New York State. That file has one row
per person, almost 200,000 people, with their age, sex,
marital status, whether they married or divorced in the past
year, their education, and their income. Each person has a
weight, so when I add them up they represent the full 19.5
million people in New York.

============================================================
SLIDE 4: How I did it
============================================================
My process had five steps. First I loaded the files with
pandas. Then I cleaned them: I cut the tables out of the
Excel sheets, stripped footnote numbers off the years and
state names, converted text to numbers, reshaped the state
data from wide to long, and merged everything into clean
tables. Then I described the data with summary statistics
and percent changes, and for the Census data I used the
person weights. I visualized everything with matplotlib. And
finally I tested whether the patterns were real using linear
regression, a paired t-test, a chi-square test, and a two-
sample t-test. All of it is in one Jupyter notebook on
GitHub.

============================================================
SLIDE 5: Q1: Both rates fell in the U.S., 2000 to 2017
============================================================
For the first question: from 2000 to 2017, both the marriage
rate and the divorce rate in the United States went down.
Marriages dropped from 8.2 to 6.9 per thousand people, about
16 percent. Divorces dropped from 4.0 to 2.9, about 28
percent, so divorce actually fell faster than marriage. I
ran a linear regression on each line, and both slopes are
significant with p-values under 0.001. These are real
trends, not random year-to-year noise.

============================================================
SLIDE 6: Q1: New York has fewer divorces than the U.S.
============================================================
New York followed the same downward path, but with
consistently fewer divorces than the country. In every
single one of the 18 years, New York's divorce rate was
below the U.S. rate, by about 0.6 divorces per thousand on
average. A paired t-test confirms that difference is
significant. For marriage, New York started below the U.S.
average in the early 2000s but moved above it around 2011.
By 2017 New York ranked 12th out of 51 for marriage, and
32nd out of the 46 states that report divorces.

============================================================
SLIDE 7: Q2: 2019 was right on the trend, not different
============================================================
Second question: was 2019 different? No. The CDC data stops
at 2017, so for 2019 I used the Census survey. The survey
asks each person whether they got married or divorced in the
past 12 months. Because every marriage or divorce involves
two people, I divided the counts by two, and I used the
whole population as the denominator, the same way the CDC
does. That gives New York about 6.5 marriages and 2.3
divorces per thousand people in 2019, a small step down from
7.3 and 2.7 in 2017. That is exactly what the long, slow
decline would predict. Nothing about 2019 stands out. One
more fact from that year: fewer than half of New York
adults, about 47 percent, were married, and about 9 percent
were divorced.

============================================================
SLIDE 8: Q3: Divorced vs. married New Yorkers
============================================================
Third question: what do divorced people have in common? I
compared adults who are currently divorced with adults who
are currently married. Divorced New Yorkers are about five
years older on average, 57 and a half versus 52 and a half.
Sixty percent are women, compared with fifty percent of
married adults. They earn about 14,000 dollars less per year
on average, and a t-test shows that gap is statistically
significant. They are less likely to have a bachelor's
degree, 31 percent versus 41 percent. And one in five has
been married more than once.

============================================================
SLIDE 9: Q3: Divorce peaks in the 50s and 60s
============================================================
Looking at age: I limited this to people who have ever been
married, so young adults who never married don't drag the
numbers down. The share who are currently divorced climbs
steadily: 5 percent in their twenties, about 9 percent in
their thirties, 13 and a half in their forties, and about 18
percent in their fifties and sixties. It dips after 70, but
that is mostly because widowhood takes over at that age, not
because older people divorce less.

============================================================
SLIDE 10: Q3: Education matters, but not in a straight line
============================================================
Education: I expected a straight line, more education, less
divorce, but that is not what the data shows. Adults with
some college or an associate degree have the highest share
currently divorced, almost 18 percent. Adults with a
bachelor's degree or higher have the lowest, 12 percent.
People with less than high school are in the middle. A chi-
square test confirms that education and divorce are related,
with a p-value under 0.001, so this pattern is not random.

============================================================
SLIDE 11: What I found
============================================================
So, to sum up. One: marriage and divorce both declined from
2000 to 2017, and divorce fell faster. New York had fewer
divorces than the country in every year. Two: 2019 was not
special. New York's 2019 numbers sit right on that downward
trend. Three: in New York, divorced adults are older, more
often women, earn about 14,000 dollars less, and are less
likely to have a bachelor's degree. Divorce peaks in the
fifties and sixties.

============================================================
SLIDE 12: Limitations and next steps
============================================================
A few limitations. The CDC's national divorce rate leaves
out states that don't report divorces, like California. The
CDC data ends in 2017 and the Census data is just 2019, so
they measure slightly different things. The Census tells me
who is divorced right now, not who got divorced, so someone
who divorced and remarried counts as married. And these are
patterns, not causes. Being older or earning less does not
cause divorce; a divorce can also cause lower income,
especially for women. If I kept going, I would add the CDC
data through 2023 to see what the pandemic did to the trend,
and use several years of Census data to follow the same age
groups over time.
